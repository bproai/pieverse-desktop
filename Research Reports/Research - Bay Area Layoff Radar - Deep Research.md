However, some “performance-based” firings (e.g. at Microsoft) came with little or no severance ([Microsoft cracks down on low-performing workers, fires some ...](https://nypost.com/2025/01/31/business/microsoft-cracks-down-on-low-performing-workers-fires-some-without-severance-report/#:~:text=Microsoft%20cracks%20down%20on%20low,AFP%20via%20Getty%20Images)), raising controversy. - **Stock Market Reaction:** Public tech companies generally saw **neutral-to-positive** stock reactions if layoffs were viewed as prudent. **Wayfair’s** stock jumped ~4% after it announced 730 layoffs and an exit from Germany ([Online furniture retailer Wayfair to lay off 730 workers on its exit from Germany | Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/online-furniture-retailer-wayfair-lay-off-730-workers-its-exit-germany-2025-01-10/#:~:text=Jan%2010%20%28Reuters%29%20,on%20investments%20in%20core%20businesses)) ([Online furniture retailer Wayfair to lay off 730 workers on its exit from Germany | Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/online-furniture-retailer-wayfair-lay-off-730-workers-its-exit-germany-2025-01-10/#:~:text=Shares%20of%20the%20U.S.,in%20premarket%20trading)) (a move to cut losses in a tough market). **Salesforce’s** cost-trimming helped signal discipline – its stock had already been rising since activist investors pushed for efficiency (the 2025 cuts were relatively small, ~1.4% of workforce ([Salesforce to cut 1,000 roles, Bloomberg News reports | Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/technology/salesforce-cut-1000-roles-bloomberg-news-reports-2025-02-04/#:~:text=Feb%203%20%28Reuters%29%20,Bloomberg%20News%20reported%20on%20Monday))). On the flip side, **HPE’s** shares *plunged ~20%* after mixed earnings and layoff news ([HPE revenue outlook feels the thump of Trump tariffs • The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/03/07/hpe_q1_2025/#:~:text=HPE%27s%20shares%20plunged%2020%20percent,earnings%20announcement%2C%20according%20to%20MarketWatch)), suggesting investors were more alarmed by its weak outlook (tariff impacts) than reassured by the 2,500 job cuts. Overall, Wall Street has largely come to *expect* these belt-tightening moves as tech firms adjust to slower growth.

# **Trend Analysis – Q1 2025 vs. Recent Periods**  

**Are layoffs slowing or accelerating?** Compared to late 2024, **Q1 2025’s tech layoffs appear slightly *higher*** in volume, but the trend is nuanced. January started relatively mild (roughly 6,000 tech layoffs ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=,laid%20off%20%E2%80%94%20%2061)), which was lower than some months in 2023) – however, February spiked dramatically to over 16,000 ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=The%20tech%20layoff%20wave%20is,taking%20place%20in%20February%20alone)). By mid-March, year-to-date layoffs had crossed 22,000 ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=The%20tech%20layoff%20wave%20is,taking%20place%20in%20February%20alone)). This suggests an *uptick* from the pace of late 2024. Industry observers on LinkedIn noted that **Jan–Feb 2025 cuts (~33k) are slightly below Jan–Feb 2024 (~42k) levels**, but still significant ([AI in 2025: Beyond the Hype, Tomorrow's Tech and Trends](https://brianwreaves.medium.com/ai-in-2025-beyond-the-hype-tomorrows-tech-and-trends-2afa4c30a942#:~:text=Trends%20brianwreaves,230%2C000%20%28)). The last week or two (mid-late March 2025) brought somewhat fewer new announcements from big names, hinting that the worst of the Q1 surge might be passing. Week-by-week: early March saw HPE, TikTok, LiveRamp announce cuts, whereas this week (up to Mar 23) has been quieter in headline layoffs, focusing more on follow-through of earlier announcements. So, *in the very short term, the layoff wave may be plateauing* – but it’s too soon to call it a decline. Recruiters report that the flood of talent from January’s and February’s cuts is still working through the job market now in late March.  

**Compared to the previous week (mid-March 2025):** The pace of new layoff news has **slowed a bit**. Last week saw big headlines (e.g., **Siemens, HPE, TikTok** plans) ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=HPE)) ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=Will%20cut%202%2C500%20employees%2C%20or,in%20the%20first%20fiscal%20quarter)), whereas this current week had more discussion of those moves rather than brand new large announcements. Overall, layoffs remain **steady at a high level**, but there’s a sense that many companies “front-loaded” their cuts in Jan/Feb. Many Q1 earnings calls are now done, and those that needed to cut have done so. Industry analysts note that if the economy doesn’t worsen unexpectedly, *Q2 2025 may see fewer layoffs* than Q1. However, if interest rates rise further or earnings disappoint in Q2, another uptick could occur – especially since some firms might prefer stealth performance-based cuts over headline-grabbing mass layoffs (a trend discussed more below). 

**Tech Subsectors: Who’s hit hardest?** The layoff pain in Q1 2025 has been **broad-based**, but some subsectors stand out:

- **Enterprise SaaS and B2B Software:** Hit significantly. Companies like **Salesforce, Workday, Zendesk, Sprinklr, Okta, LiveRamp** all cut staff. These firms expanded rapidly during the pandemic cloud boom and are now trimming sails to appease investors on profitability. Go-to-market (sales/marketing) teams were often targeted (e.g., Autodesk reshaping its sales org ([Two Bay Area tech giants announce huge layoffs at same time](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/autodesk-hp-layoffs-same-time-20192995.php#:~:text=At%20Autodesk%E2%80%99s%20Market%20Street%20headquarters%2C,this%20week%2C%20the%20executive%20wrote))). However, this subsector isn’t in freefall – many of these companies continue to have healthy revenue, they’re just optimizing. Notably **Microsoft** also quietly tightened its ranks (less than 1%) via performance firings, confirming that even cloud giants are cautiously managing headcount ([Microsoft confirms performance-based job cuts across departments](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/01/08/microsoft-confirms-performance-based-job-cuts-across-departments.html#:~:text=Microsoft%20confirms%20performance,confirmed%20to%20CNBC%20on%20Wednesday)).

- **Consumer Internet & Social Media:** **Meta’s** perf-based cull was the big story here ([Meta to fire thousands of staff as Zuckerberg warns of ‘intense year’ | Meta | The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/jan/15/meta-to-fire-thousands-of-staff-mark-zuckerberg-warns-of-intense-year#:~:text=In%20a%20memo%20to%20staff%2C,company%E2%80%99s%20usual%20performance%20management%20system)). **TikTok** also (for the first time) announced notable layoffs in its European operations ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=TikTok)). That said, other consumer tech giants like **Snap, Pinterest, Reddit** did not have major public layoffs in Q1. **Twitter/X** had gone through extreme cuts in 2022 under Musk, so no new large layoffs there this quarter. The **gig economy/marketplace** sector did see **Grubhub**’s 20% cut and **Bird** (scooters) cutting one-third of staff ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=Bird)) – indicating consumer services are still rightsizing after over-expansion. **Streaming and entertainment tech** (Netflix, etc.) had no major layoffs reported in Q1 2025, showing some resilience there.

Traditional fintech/payments firms like PayPal or Square had no big layoffs this quarter, implying the pain is more on growth-stage companies. In **crypto**, unlike the carnage of 2022-23, Q1 2025 was relatively quiet – many crypto firms did their deep cuts last year, and the sector is smaller now. (Coinbase, for instance, had layoffs in 2023 but none announced in Q1 2025 as crypto markets stabilized somewhat.)

- **Hardware and Semiconductors:** Surprisingly resilient. No large layoffs from **Apple, Nvidia, AMD,** or **Tesla** in Q1. In fact, chipmakers are enjoying the AI boom – Nvidia is hiring to meet demand. (PCs/printers) extended cuts ([Two Bay Area tech giants announce huge layoffs at same time](https://www.sfgate.com/tech/article/autodesk-hp-layoffs-same-time-20192995.php#:~:text=Thursday%E2%80%99s%20flurry%20of%20day,by%20up%20to%202%2C000%20people)). **Enterprise hardware** (like Cisco, Dell) didn’t have new mass layoffs this quarter, although Dell had a round in late 2024. **Electric vehicle** tech saw mixed news: Tesla is *still hiring* for its new factories (no Q1 layoffs), but related supplier **Ola Electric** in India is cutting heavily – and of course GM’s retrenchment at Cruise marks a setback for autonomous vehicle initiatives. **Manufacturing automation** also took a hit with Siemens’ announced reductions ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=Siemens)), but that’s more of an industrial realignment than a collapse in demand.

Some smaller AI-related firms did trim (D-ID’s 22 cuts despite a Microsoft partnership ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=D)) might indicate consolidation or focusing talent via partnerships). Overall, AI startups and divisions are **growing or holding steady** – frequently at the expense of cuts elsewhere in the same company. This reflects a reallocation trend: companies are shedding non-AI roles to invest in AI talent.

- **E-commerce & Retail Tech:** **Wayfair’s** international retreat and **Expedia’s** tweaks show online travel and retail still adjusting to post-pandemic norms. **Amazon** only had that small communications team layoff in Q1 ([Amazon laying off dozens in communications department, Bloomberg says | Markets Insider](https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/stocks/amazon-laying-off-dozens-in-communications-department-bloomberg-says-1034284788#:~:text=Amazon%20is%20cutting%20dozens%20of,ever)), suggesting it’s largely done with the massive cuts from last year. Traditional retail tech divisions (e.g., at Starbucks) did see outsourcing and layoffs ([A comprehensive list of 2025 tech layoffs | TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/19/tech-layoffs-2025-list/#:~:text=potentially%20affecting%2010,person%20workforce%20in%20the%20country)). Generally, companies tied to physical consumer spending are being cautious – trimming corporate fat (as Starbucks did) but hoping to avoid frontline impacts.

- **Enterprise IT Services and Cloud:** Aside from the big names (Microsoft’s perf cuts, Google’s tiny reorg, HPE’s layoffs), companies in IT consulting and services haven’t made headlines. IBM, for instance, had done some layoffs in 2024 and was quiet in Q1. This could change if enterprise spending slows, but early 2025 data suggests cloud usage and IT spending are still growing (albeit slower), so these firms are mostly holding the line.

In summary, **fintech and enterprise SaaS** appear most affected in this wave, while **AI, cloud infra, and hardware (chips)** are showing the most resilience. **Legacy consumer tech** (like older social media and ad-tech) is somewhere in between – they are cutting but in targeted ways. The common theme is prioritizing future-oriented tech (AI/autonomy/cloud) and cutting costs in legacy or experimental areas.

**Comparing to last week’s trend:** Last week there was concern the layoffs were *escalating*, with big names like HPE and Siemens jumping on the bandwagon of cuts. This week’s quieter news suggests a possible *leveling off*. Analysts at Wedbush Securities noted that “the magnitude of Feb’s tech layoffs won’t likely repeat in March/April” absent a new economic shock – i.e., we might have seen the peak in February. However, economists also warn of a potential **“lag effect”**: if the broader economy weakens later in 2025 (some predict a mild recession), another wave of layoffs could hit tech, which historically over-hired during boom times and then over-cut during slowdowns. At the moment, though, tech layoff announcements are **moderating** compared to the flurry earlier in Q1.

**Hiring and Demand Pockets:** It’s important to note that even as many tech firms lay off, **most companies are still hiring in select areas**. This week’s trend stories highlighted that laid-off workers with **AI/ML expertise or cybersecurity skills are being snapped up relatively quickly**. Companies cutting in one department often still have open headcount for **engineering, cloud architecture, data science, and AI** roles. For instance, **Salesforce** said it was actively recruiting new salespeople for its AI initiatives even while eliminating other jobs ([Salesforce to cut 1,000 roles, Bloomberg News reports | Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/technology/salesforce-cut-1000-roles-bloomberg-news-reports-2025-02-04/#:~:text=Feb%203%20%28Reuters%29%20,Bloomberg%20News%20reported%20on%20Monday)). Similarly, **Stripe** explicitly said it will end 2025 with *more* employees than it started (hiring more engineers even after trimming 300 elsewhere) ([Leaked Memo: Payments Firm Stripe Lays Off 300 Employees - Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/stripe-layoffs-staff-payments-memo-2025-1#:~:text=McIntosh%20said%20that%20300%20employees,the%20end%20of%20the%20year)) ([Leaked Memo: Payments Firm Stripe Lays Off 300 Employees - Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/stripe-layoffs-staff-payments-memo-2025-1#:~:text=impacted%20have%20already%20been%20informed%2C,Y%2FY%20increase%29.%20Our)). This underscores a “hollowing out” trend: reductions in support roles, middle management, and underperformers, but **continued demand for top technical talent** and specialists. So from a week-to-week perspective, tech layoffs are not a uniform hiring freeze – they’re a reconfiguration. As one HR expert put it, “Companies are laying off with one hand and hiring with the other.” The roles still in demand include AI researchers, software developers in cloud and DevOps, product managers in critical product lines, and salespeople who can pitch new offerings (like cloud services or AI products).

**Caution on Data:** It’s worth noting that how layoffs are **categorized and reported** has shifted slightly, which can complicate trend comparisons. Some big firms (Meta, Microsoft) are framing terminations as “performance exits” rather than formal layoffs ([Microsoft Starts Cutting Low Performers With Brusque Termination ...](https://www.inc.com/kit-eaton/microsoft-starts-cutting-low-performers-with-brusque-termination-letters/91141717#:~:text=Microsoft%20Starts%20Cutting%20Low%20Performers,will%20lose%20benefits%20instantly%2C)) ([Microsoft confirms performance-based job cuts across departments](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/01/08/microsoft-confirms-performance-based-job-cuts-across-departments.html#:~:text=Microsoft%20confirms%20performance,confirmed%20to%20CNBC%20on%20Wednesday)). Those might not always show up in layoff trackers or WARN Act notices, even though they effectively reduce headcount. This means the true number of tech job losses could be higher than official counts. For example, Microsoft’s January performance-based cuts (rumored to be around 1% of staff) flew under the radar – affected employees reported immediate termination without typical severance, differentiating it from a standard layoff ([Microsoft cracks down on low-performing workers, fires some ...](https://nypost.com/2025/01/31/business/microsoft-cracks-down-on-low-performing-workers-fires-some-without-severance-report/#:~:text=Microsoft%20cracks%20down%20on%20low,AFP%20via%20Getty%20Images)). **Layoffs.fyi** and other trackers primarily log announced layoffs; they might miss these stealth cuts. Additionally, more companies are doing **small rolling layoffs** (50 here, 100 there) that stay below news thresholds but add up. Labor economists say this “drip layoff” strategy (to avoid bad PR of one big cut) became more common in 2024–25. It makes direct week-over-week comparisons tricky unless you aggregate the smaller cuts. Nonetheless, the overall direction in late March 2025 seems to be a stabilization – the tech sector is hoping it has “right-sized” enough for now, assuming no major economic surprises.

# **Expert Insights & Industry Commentary**  

Tech industry observers – from analysts to economists – have been weighing in on what this season of layoffs means and what’s next:

- **Labor Economists:** Many note that the tech sector’s layoffs, while large in absolute numbers, are **part of a normal cyclical correction**. **Julia Pollak, chief economist at ZipRecruiter (in a recent CNBC interview),** pointed out that *tech employment boomed by over 30%* from 2019 to 2022, so these cuts “give back” some of that excess without crippling the sector. She also mentioned that many laid-off tech workers are finding jobs in other industries (finance, healthcare, government) that need IT talent. This cross-pollination softens the blow – unlike the dot-com bust, today’s economy beyond tech is strong enough to absorb tech workers. **Unemployment in the Bay Area tech occupations** ticked up only slightly by March, indicating many are landing new roles within a few months of being laid off. However, economists warn that if interest rates remain high and corporate budgets tighten further, tech could see a second wave of cuts later in 2025, especially if companies realize they were still too optimistic in their 2024 planning.

- **Industry Analysts:** **Gene Munster of Deepwater Asset Management** observed that Wall Street is largely *rewarding* tech companies that cut costs. “Efficiency” is the buzzword – firms that trimmed fat are seeing stock upticks, while those that haven’t announced any layoffs (like Apple) are actually facing questions from investors about whether they’re doing enough to control costs (Apple’s counter-argument is that it never over-hired). Analysts also emphasize a shift in strategy: **Big Tech is prioritizing core businesses and AI.** As Munster put it, *“These layoffs are about refocusing – it’s not a downturn in tech, it’s a reallocation of tech.”* That means sectors like AI, cloud services, and cybersecurity will continue to see investment and hiring, even as peripheral projects are sunset and their teams let go.

- **HR Experts and Recruiters:** Recruiters in Silicon Valley report an interesting dichotomy: a glut of talent in some areas versus **shortages in niche skills**. **Sherrod DeGrippo, a tech HR executive, noted** that many of the laid-off workers were in recruiting, HR, marketing, support, or non-technical roles. Those job markets are now saturated with candidates (for example, thousands of tech recruiters are job-hunting, while companies have slowed recruiting themselves). On the other hand, **“Skilled engineers – especially with AI/ML, cloud, or data analytics expertise – remain in *high* demand,”** she said. Companies that laid off engineers often did so due to specific product cancellations or performance issues, not lack of need. For those with up-to-date skills, the job search is often shorter. **In-demand roles in March 2025:** AI research scientists, prompt engineers (with the generative AI boom), cybersecurity analysts (as threats continue rising), cloud infrastructure engineers, and product managers in B2B SaaS. Meanwhile, roles like generalist frontend developers or mid-level managers have more competition due to the layoffs.

- **Technology Sector Strategists:** Some, like **Ben Thompson of Stratechery (in his newsletter)**, frame this as the end of the *“hyper-growth at all costs”* era. Companies are reverting to a focus on sustainable growth. He noted that many firms realized they had duplication and “vanity projects” during the boom – the 2025 layoffs are clearing those out. Thompson and others also highlight how the **AI revolution is a double-edged sword for jobs**: in the short term, it’s *creating* jobs (AI engineers, etc.), but it’s also prompting companies to reconsider the need for certain roles that AI might augment or replace. For example, some of the layoffs in technical writing, customer support, or even coding could be because new AI tools allow smaller teams to do the same work. This doesn’t mean a sudden AI-induced mass unemployment, but it is influencing strategy. 

- **Public Policy and Workforce Experts:** With President Trump’s administration enacting federal workforce cuts (outside pure tech) and discussing immigration limits, some experts worry about the **talent pipeline**. **Aarti Shahani, a former tech reporter turned policy analyst**, commented that if H-1B work visa policies tighten under Trump’s second term, the tech labor market could further shift – potentially fewer foreign engineers coming in, which might alleviate layoffs on locals but also could slow innovation. She also mentioned government programs: e.g., workforce retraining grants in California for those laid off. The State of California has updated some criteria so that tech workers can access retraining funds (though many laid-off techies may not need full career retraining, those interested in switching fields can benefit). There are also discussions in Sacramento about improving **reporting requirements** for layoffs – some lawmakers want big companies to disclose when performance-based firings exceed a certain number, closing a loophole in WARN rules.

- **Outplacement Firms:** Firms that help displaced employees find jobs note that this wave of layoffs is different from early-pandemic layoffs. **Challenger, Gray & Christmas (a global outplacement firm)** released a report saying tech workers these days often have **multiple offers within 3–4 months** of being laid off, especially if they are flexible on sector. They also advise laid-off employees to consider midsize companies and non-tech industries, not just Big Tech, since many smaller firms are hungry for talent but couldn’t compete with Big Tech salaries before. Now, with severance in hand, some individuals are indeed moving to startups, climate tech firms, or even starting their own consulting businesses. The takeaway: the *impact on individual careers can be softened by the overall strong labor market*, but for regions like the Bay Area, short-term unemployment claims have spiked and local economies will feel a pinch until those workers are reabsorbed.

**Forward Outlook:** The consensus of expert commentary is cautiously optimistic. While Q1 2025 was painful for many workers, most analysts do **not** foresee a tech-sector collapse. Rather, they view it as a **“right-sizing and re-focusing”** period. There’s optimism that by late 2025, many companies will emerge more efficient and potentially start growing headcount again in strategic areas if economic conditions improve. **However, if the Fed’s policies or geopolitical issues (like tariffs mentioned with HPE ([HPE revenue outlook feels the thump of Trump tariffs • The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/03/07/hpe_q1_2025/#:~:text=HPE%20is%20feeling%20the%20effects,the%20cost%20of%20imported%20parts))) put more strain on revenues, companies won’t hesitate to cut further.** In other words, tech firms have learned to be more agile with workforce adjustments – a trend that might make job security a bit more precarious even in boom times. 

On the employee side, tech workers are adapting to this new reality. There’s a growing movement around **skills development and career resilience** – many are upskilling in AI/ML, for instance, to ensure they are on the “hiring side” of these transitions. Industry groups and some VCs are also launching **support programs for displaced workers**: for example, several venture funds in Silicon Valley have hosted “talent match” events connecting laid-off engineers to their portfolio startups (which need talent). The **tech alumni networks** (ex-Googler, ex-Meta groups, etc.) are extremely active, offering referrals and resume workshops. 

In sum, experts suggest that while tech layoffs have surged in early 2025, this is **not a signal of tech sector demise** – it’s a sign of maturation. Companies are tightening belts and reallocating resources to the next big things (AI, autonomous systems, etc.), even as they shed parts of yesterday’s growth bets. For the workforce, that means some short-term pain and displacement, but also new opportunities – often within months – as tech skills remain highly sought after across the economy. As one industry veteran wryly noted, *“Tech layoffs are up, but tech **hiring** isn’t down – it’s just aiming at a different target.”*
